# Visual Storytelling

Make each stage change what the viewer understands. Sequence should resolve questions, reveal relationships, or support exploration rather than repeat the same proposition with different effects.

## Define the knowledge progression

Identify what the viewer knows on arrival, what they need to understand, and what action or interpretation the experience should enable. Order stages by unresolved questions: what is this, where am I, why does it matter, how does it work, what can I inspect, and what can I do next?

Introduction, orientation, focal presentation, discovery, exploration, detail, transformation, information, and action are possible roles, not a required sequence. A simple product may need only an orienting view and a direct action. A returning user may need to bypass introductory material entirely.

For each retained stage, define its new information, focal subject, evidence, relevant action, and transition to the next stage. A claim needs a visible demonstration, relevant detail, comparison, or supporting content; spectacle alone does not establish it. Keep measured facts, inferred mechanisms, and conceptual illustration distinguishable. Merge stages that communicate the same information unless repetition has a deliberate learning or comparison role.

## Choose the navigation model

| Need | Useful model | Constraint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Read an argument | Continuous content flow | Keep evidence beside the claim it supports |
| Explain several features of one subject | Stable subject with changing annotation or detail | Release the composition when it no longer fits the content |
| Teach a dependency | Ordered stages with visible progress | Permit review and preserve a coherent overview |
| Explore independent details | Branching selection | Keep a clear return and visited or current context |
| Compare states | Side-by-side or directly switchable views | Preserve shared scale, framing, and labels |
| Reveal a spatial relationship | Motivated viewpoint or object change | Retain orientation through landmarks and stable anchors |

Do not require a cinematic journey where direct comparison is the task. A narrative can coexist with a faster task path.

## Choreograph the reveal

Establish context before revealing a detail whose meaning depends on it. Show cause before effect where causality matters. Keep the important subject stable long enough to be perceived, and introduce only the information needed for the current question. A continuous morph can imply a real process or equivalence; use it only when that implication is supported or clearly conceptual.

Give each key state a complete composition: recognizable subject, dominant relationship, readable negative space, and enough context to orient the viewer. Alternate overview and detail, density and restraint, or movement and stillness when the information benefits from that change in rhythm. Do not make every stage a new visual climax.

Use motion to connect stages that share identity or space. Use a cut when continuous travel would consume time without useful information. Coordinate the subject event, annotation, and camera around one focal relationship. Keep identity, relative scale, and labels consistent across a comparison; disclose intentional changes in framing or magnification that could alter its meaning.

Employ [motion intelligence](../motion-intelligence/SKILL.md) for temporal behavior and [camera composition](../camera-composition/SKILL.md) for motivated shots. Use [assembly choreography](../assembly-choreography/SKILL.md) when the explanation depends on physical release and attachment.

## Preserve user control

For an authored narrative controlled by navigation progress, map progress to meaningful states with stable boundaries. Derive the subject pose, camera, annotations, and current-stage indicator from the same progress reference. Reverse movement, direct jumps, resumption, and changed content dimensions should resolve to coherent states. Do not rely on accumulated movement deltas as the only record of where the viewer is.

Keep ordinary reading and navigation predictable. Unexplained input with no visible response feels broken; a deliberate reading hold should retain ordinary navigation and a clear next step. Shorten empty travel and keep skip, revisit, and direct task access discoverable.

If manual object inspection interrupts a scripted sequence, define who controls the subject and how the viewer returns. A direct jump must establish the destination's context without requiring every preceding animation to play.

## Adapt the same meaning

For limited space or reduced movement, use selected key states, annotated stills, stacked explanation, or direct view switching. Preserve the information and action sequence while reducing travel and simultaneous content.

A spatial story's alternative needs to explain the relevant relationships; a decorative poster is insufficient when hidden construction or relative position is the lesson. Use [responsive composition](../responsive-composition/SKILL.md) and [visual accessibility](../visual-accessibility/SKILL.md) for these adaptations.

## Diagnose narrative weakness

If stages feel repetitive, identify what new knowledge each supplies. If the viewer loses orientation, recover a landmark or overview. If the story feels slow, remove empty intervals before accelerating all events. If the primary action becomes hard to reach, shorten or bypass the introduction. If the explanation requires continuous animation to be intelligible, strengthen the meaningful states themselves.

Keep a compact stage map in the existing specification: the viewer's question, new information, focal composition, evidence or annotation, entry and exit conditions, and available action. Include direct-jump context and alternatives for reduced movement or limited space where needed.
