# Camera Composition

Treat each view as an answer to a question about the subject. Define what the viewer must understand before choosing position, projection, or movement.

## Choose the relationship to the subject

Identify the primary feature, useful secondary context, intended apparent scale, viewing angle, horizon, and region reserved for text or controls. A low view can emphasize stature; a high view can reveal arrangement. Select the angle that explains the requested relationship without obscuring actionable geometry.

Use perspective when near-far size relationships and physical presence matter. Use parallel projection for consistent scale with depth or diagrammatic clarity; orientation still affects projected length through foreshortening.

Camera position determines perspective relationships. A wider field of view from the same position shows more of the scene; moving closer to restore subject size exaggerates near-far differences. A narrower field paired with a more distant position can flatten apparent depth. Do not attribute all distortion or compression to field of view alone.

Choose the viewpoint that separates the subject's identifying contours and explains its most important connection. A dramatic angle that hides a defining opening, articulation, or marking may misrepresent the object. For comparisons, maintain a common projection, scale policy, and orientation unless an intentional difference is clearly explained.

## Fit against usable space

Establish the subject bounds in its required pose, including protrusions, motion extent, and attached parts. Fit against the actual free composition region after overlays and safe margins, not the entire output rectangle.

For perspective views, both horizontal and vertical angular extent constrain fit; subject depth and off-center placement also matter. A front-facing width-and-height estimate is only an initial bound. Inspect projected extremities of irregular or deep subjects and account for the nearest parts becoming disproportionately large.

For parallel views, frame size controls apparent scale; changing distance alone does not provide the same fitting behavior. In either projection, update framing when the available region changes.

Place depth clipping boundaries outside all needed geometry and viewpoint paths while avoiding unnecessarily extreme range. Inspect the near parts of a tilted object, large exploded assemblies, and closest permitted inspection positions. A correct initial view does not guarantee unclipped travel.

Refine visual balance after geometric fit. Breathing room, a dark feature, or caption weight may justify offsetting the subject from the center of the usable region.

Use negative space as a shaped part of the shot: an open interval can receive a subject's direction, a future movement, or the visual weight of copy. Give crops clear intent. An assured crop through a broad mass can feel closer and more deliberate than a tiny margin beside a protruding tip; required distinguishing features still need to survive. Resolve silhouettes that only touch the frame, horizon, or neighboring contours unless the contact carries meaning.

Depth of field can separate the subject, but the required feature needs enough depth in focus to remain intelligible. On a close product view, focus on a useful plane through the important construction rather than merely the nearest point. Excessively shallow focus can make a full-size object appear miniature or hide the accuracy the shot is supposed to demonstrate.

## Move for a reason

| Movement | Information it can reveal | Constraint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Translation toward or away | Depth and changing relation to foreground | Preserve useful subject scale and avoid passing through geometry |
| Field-of-view change | Tighter or wider framing | Do not confuse it with physical approach |
| Orbit around a target | Hidden sides and construction | Maintain the intended target and useful orientation limits |
| Lateral translation | Relative depth through parallax | Keep the relevant relationship visible |
| Pan across a scene | Continuity between separated subjects | Preserve a landmark or clear destination |
| Cut or direct view change | A substantial shift without prolonged travel | Re-establish subject identity and orientation immediately |

For a sequence, establish start frame, motivation, movement, subject event, and settling frame. Hold the destination long enough for its information to be read. Constant camera floating can weaken inspection and compete with content even when its amplitude is small.

Coordinate subject and camera movement. If both rotate simultaneously, the viewer may no longer understand which part changed. Use one dominant event or preserve a stable reference. Follow [motion intelligence](../motion-intelligence/SKILL.md) for acceleration, interruption, and control handoff.

Compose the travel path as well as its endpoints. An orbit can temporarily collapse a crucial gap, hide the selected feature, or carry a bright reflection across a label. If the path adds no necessary information, a direct change with a clear orientation cue may serve the task better. Avoid automatic reframing for every small subject movement; hold a useful composition until its protected region is threatened.

## Support inspection and return

Let direct manipulation take ownership promptly. Bound orbit, travel, zoom, and roll according to useful views; preserve a recognizable up direction unless the experience specifically requires free orientation. Provide a reset or return that restores a meaningful shot while retaining task state.

Retarget from the current view on rapid selection. Keep the selected feature visible during panel changes and travel where continuity matters. Avoid sudden orientation flips or shortest-path rotations that contradict an intended orbit.

Provide stable alternate views for reduced movement. Slower travel is not automatically a comfortable substitute for a large translation or rotation.

## Recompose across conditions

Reassess the shot for portrait, short-height, enlarged text, and open details. Change the subject's projected center, distance, projection bounds, secondary context, or panel placement as needed. Preserving identity and inspection value matters more than identical coordinates.

Inspect the initial view, important features, allowed control limits, transition path, and return. If the subject is tiny, fix available-space allocation before widening the field further. If parts look distorted, distinguish camera distance from geometry error. If the destination is clear but the journey disorients, simplify travel and strengthen landmarks.

Carry forward the shot's purpose, projection, subject emphasis, usable region, protected features, and permitted movement. Express framing in terms of apparent scale and relationships where display size varies; retain exact coordinates only where the project needs them.
